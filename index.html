Applied to a lot of jobs and never heard back?

Before a recruiter reads your resume, an AI tool may have already scored it.

You apply

You upload your resume to an online job portal.

→
The AI screens it

An AI tool reads your resume and compares it to the job.

→
The AI ranks you

You get a score or a rank. This is where bias can come in.

→
A recruiter looks

Usually only the top-ranked applicants get seen by a person.

If you never heard back, a person may never have seen your application. Many companies use AI to sort and rank resumes first because it saves them time.

The catch is that these tools learn from a company's past hiring. If that hiring favored some groups over others, the AI can pick up the same habit, and it's hard for anyone to notice.

Where the bias comes from

You can control what's on your resume, but not the tool reading it. Here's how that tool can end up being unfair, and why not hearing back isn't always about you.

The AI copies past hiring

An AI learns what a "good hire" looks like by studying who a company hired before. If those past choices were lopsided, the AI copies them.

Past hires

A company mostly hired people from one group.

→
The AI learns

"A good hire looks like the people we hired before."

→
The AI's shortlist

It keeps picking that same group, even when new applicants are equally qualified.

Group hired often in the past    Group hired less often

A simple illustration, not real data. Bias most often starts in the data the AI learns from [1][2][3]. It can also come from how the tool is built [1] or how an employer words its instructions to the AI [4].

What this means for you: If you're applying to a field that hasn't hired many people like you, an online application alone can be an uphill climb. Pair it with a way to meet people directly, like a referral or a career fair.

Same resume, different name

A name can hint at someone's gender or race. To test for bias, researchers give the AI two resumes that are identical except for the name. A fair AI would score them the same.

What they found

The names changed the scores, even with identical qualifications. [4][5] In one study, the AI models even gave fair-sounding reasons that hid the bias. [4]

Why it matters to you

Your name could affect your score, and you'd never see it happen. That's a good reason not to rely on job portals alone. A referral or an alumni connection puts your resume in front of a person first.

Even experts can't see inside most of these tools yet, so the bias often goes unnoticed. [1][2]

Your checklist for success

You can't fix a biased tool on your own, but you can make sure the AI reads your resume correctly, and find ways to get it in front of a person.
